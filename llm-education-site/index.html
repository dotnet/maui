01 / ANATOMY

Begin with the mechanism

What is an LLM,
actually?

A large language model is a neural network trained on sequences of tokens. During generation it repeatedly estimates what token is likely to come next. Scale, attention, training data, and post-training make this simple objective produce surprisingly general behavior.

THE INPUT LAYER

Tokens are not always words.

A tokenizer converts text into integer IDs from a fixed vocabulary. Common words may be one token; unusual words may split into several. Tokenization affects context limits, cost, speed, and how easily a model handles different languages.

Think of it like: movable type in a printing press.

03 / TRAINING

From raw text to useful assistant

Three different
jobs called “training.”

Pretraining builds broad language capability. Supervised fine-tuning teaches response patterns. Preference optimization shapes behavior. They use different data, objectives, and amounts of compute.

A

PRETRAINING

Learn statistical structure

Predict missing or next tokens across a large corpus. The model absorbs patterns in language, code, facts, and style.

INPUTWeb, books, code, papers
OBJECTIVELower prediction error
B

SUPERVISED FINE-TUNING

Learn to follow instructions

Train on curated prompt-response examples so the base model answers in a useful conversational format.

INPUTDemonstrations
OBJECTIVEImitate good answers
C

PREFERENCE OPTIMIZATION

Shape choices and boundaries

Use human or model preferences through methods such as RLHF or DPO to favor helpful and safer responses.

INPUTRanked responses
OBJECTIVEPrefer desired behavior
COMMON MISCONCEPTION

“The model stores a copy of the internet.”

Training compresses statistical patterns into parameters. Models can memorize some examples, but they do not normally retrieve a clean source document from their weights—and they can confidently reconstruct something false.

04 / LAB — SAMPLING

Temperature is not creativity

Change the
shape of chance.

Temperature rescales token probabilities before sampling. Lower values concentrate probability on likely tokens; higher values flatten the distribution. The underlying knowledge does not improve—it only becomes more or less predictable.

Useful AI is
a system, not a prompt.

Production applications surround the model with retrieval, tools, structured outputs, guardrails, observability, and evaluation. The architecture depends on the problem—not on whichever technique is newest.

07 / LIMITS & EVALUATION

        

          

            
Fluency is not reliability

            
Know where the
confidence ends.

          

          

            LLM output should be treated as a proposal whose reliability depends on the model, task, evidence, and checks.
            Evaluation must resemble the real use case and include failures—not just average benchmark scores.
          

        


        

          
HALLUCINATION
Plausible ≠ true
Generation optimizes likely continuation, not factual verification. Ask for citations, retrieve evidence, and verify consequential claims.

          
BIAS
Data leaves fingerprints
Training and feedback data encode social and selection biases. Test outcomes across groups and contexts, not just one happy path.

          
PROMPT INJECTION
Instructions can conflict
Untrusted text can attempt to override system instructions. Separate data from authority and constrain tools with least privilege.

          
DATA EXPOSURE
Inputs may be sensitive
Choose deployment and retention policies deliberately. Minimize data, redact secrets, and understand provider terms.
